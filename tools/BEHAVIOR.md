## Nov 27 2026: ocean ships sail at their era's speeds and wait in port
- What changed: ships on the ocean routes no longer all cross in the same 220 s. Each leg is timed from its real distance and the ship's speed, they slow near harbours, and on arrival they sit in port before sailing back. Each departure picks sail or steam by the year (no steam before 1840, about a quarter by 1870, half by 1880, 80% by 1910, all by 1930), so in the late 1800s brown sailing ships and dark steamers share the same route at different speeds. Sail stays about 12 days in port (shown compressed); port stays shrink toward about a day for modern container ships.
- Ships are now tappable (before, nothing happened) and labelled with what they are doing: "under sail at about 5 knots, London to New York, day 9 of about 27", "steaming at about 9 knots ...", "slow steaming to save fuel at about 18 knots ...", "in port at Lagos, loading and unloading (about 12 days)". Labels: Sailing ship, Steamship, Cargo ship (1950-2007), Container ship (2008 on).
- Numbers and sources:
  - Nelson's passage to the West Indies and back averaged about 4 knots; New York-Liverpool packets 1818-1832 took 21-29 days, about 100-140 miles a day or 4-6 knots. Royal Museums Greenwich, Caird Library: https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/maritime-history/library-archive/18th-century-sailing-times-between-english-channel-coast
  - Average port time about 12 days per voyage for c.1840 coastal sailing ships (average voyage 31.65 days). Cambridge Group for the History of Population, "Speedier delivery": https://www.campop.geog.cam.ac.uk/research/projects/transport/coastalspeed.pdf
  - Steam's share of arrivals: nearly half by the 1870s, over 80% by 1910; listed steamer share 15.5% (1860), 24.0% (1870), 47.1% (1880); mean steam speed about 16.9 km/h (about 9 knots); steam cut voyage times 61-70%. Galofré-Vilà, Bogart, Álvarez-Palau, "From Sail to Steam" (2025 draft): https://economics.ucr.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/From-Sail-to-Steam.pdf
  - Since 2007 lines cut container ship commercial speed from 22-23 knots to about 18 knots or slower. Rodrigue/Notteboom, Port Economics, Management and Policy: https://porteconomicsmanagement.org/pemp/contents/part6/ports-and-container-shipping/slow-steaming-container-shipping/
  - Median time in port for container ships in 2023: 0.7 days (first half) and 1.1 days (second half). UNCTAD Review of Maritime Transport 2024, ch. 4: https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2024ch4_en.pdf
- Simplifications: voyages run at 1.2 s per real hour, port stays at 0.3 s per real hour so ships don't sit in harbour for minutes. Each ship's speed varies +/-15%. Routes are still straight great circles (real routes are longer). Steamer speed 1840-1949 is one flat 9 knots; 1950-1967 is a straight blend from 9 to 22.5 knots, and steam-era port time is a straight blend from 12 days (1850) to under a day (2000); both blends are not from a study. The sail/steam mix uses the Barcelona arrivals data for the whole world.

## Nov 27 2026: whales and dolphins surface to breathe
- What changed: near the camera, humpbacks, blue whales, generic whales, orcas and dolphins now follow a dive cycle instead of gliding at one depth. They rise nose-up to the surface, breathe, then dive nose-down. Big whales blow misty spouts (a white puff that rises from the head and fades), several per surfacing; humpbacks (and the generic "whale") lift their flukes before a deep dive. Orcas take one breath per surfacing; dolphins pop up every 20-40 s. Far away whales keep the old cheap glide.
- Tap a whale or dolphin: "on a deep feeding dive (most last 4 to 6 min)", "coming up to breathe", "at the surface, blowing", "lifting its flukes to dive deep", "surfacing for a breath", joined to any hunt or interaction it is doing ("chasing a sea turtle, surfacing for a breath"). Sea hunters now also show "hunting" (it was being blanked).
- Numbers and sources:
  - Humpback deep feeding dives last 4-6 min; humpback blow is bushy and 10-16 ft (3-5 m) high; fin whale blow over 40 ft. NOAA Stellwagen Bank NMS, Whale Behaviors: https://stellwagen.noaa.gov/visit/whalewatching/whale-behaviors.html
  - Humpback tail straight up before a deeper, longer dive; adults breathe on average every 10-15 min, calves every 3-5 min. ADF&G whale behaviors: https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/viewing/pdfs/whale_behaviors.pdf
  - Blue whale mean dive time 9.6 +/- 0.31 min (35 dives, Trincomalee Bay). Nanayakkara et al. 2014: https://arabianseawhalenetwork.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/nanayakkara-et-al-2014-blue-whale-surface-and-dive-times-sri-lanka-1.pdf
  - Blue whale blow 25-30 ft (about 9 m), slender, lasts up to 5 s. ADF&G blue whale notebook: https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/blue_whale.pdf ; NPS: https://www.nps.gov/places/blue-whale.htm
  - Orcas: most dives last under a minute and they take a single breath between dives (11 tagged orcas, 8,118 dives; Volpov, Trites et al., PLOS ONE). Smithsonian summary: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/between-dives-orcas-take-only-a-single-breath-180984444/
  - Coastal bottlenose dolphins dive for 20-40 s. Wild Dolphin Project: https://www.wilddolphinproject.org/how-long-can-dolphins-hold-their-breath/
- Simplifications: dives longer than a minute are shown at 1/5 of real length so a viewer can see a full cycle (humpback 48-72 s for 4-6 min, blue whale about 2 min for 9.6 min); orca and dolphin dives are real length. The number of blows per surfacing (3 for big whales, a few seconds apart) and the orca puff size are not from a study. Spout heights scale with body length, so they are exaggerated along with the meshes.

## Nov 27 2026: airliners fly real flight profiles by era
- What changed: planes take off from a city, climb, cruise, descend into the destination and wait at the gate before the next leg, instead of a fixed arc. The nose pitches up on the climb and down on the descent. Cruise height and speed follow the era: under about 3,000 m before pressurized airliners (to 1945), about 7,500 m for pressurized propeller airliners (1946-1957), about 10,700 m (35,000 ft) for jets (1958 on). Propeller flights take longer than jet flights over the same distance.
- Daily rhythm: short and medium flights (under 4,000 km) don't leave between 11pm and 6am local time at the departure city (a night curfew like Sydney Airport's), so fewer planes are in the air over the night side of the planet. Long-haul overnight flights still go.
- Tap a plane: what it is doing ("climbing out of London", "cruising at about 10,700 m to Paris", "descending into Rome") and its route. Ground labels show the same.
- Numbers and sources:
  - Airliners before the Boeing 307 flew no higher than about 3,000 m; the 307 (first pressurized airliner, 1940) could cruise at 7,500 m. Smithsonian NASM, "Making the Modern Airliner": https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/making-modern-airliner
  - Boeing 247 flew 50% faster than the Ford Tri-Motor; DC-3 crossed the US in under 16 hours (same NASM page). Used for the 1920s-50s speeds (165 and 250 km/h average including stops).
  - 707 entered service 26 Oct 1958; Pan Am New York-Paris took 8 h 41 min with a stop (about 670 km/h average). Britannica: https://britannica.com/technology/Boeing-707
  - Descent: 3 NM of travel per 1,000 ft (about a 3 degree path; FL350 starts down about 105 NM out). Rule of three: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(aeronautics); FAA Instrument Procedures Handbook ch. 3: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/instrument_procedures_handbook/FAA-H-8083-16B_Chapter_3.pdf
  - Night curfew 11pm-6am (Sydney Airport Curfew Act 1995): https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/infrastructure-transport-vehicles/aviation/aviation-safety/aircraft-noise/airport-curfews/sydney/overview
- Simplifications: the climb mirrors the 3-to-1 descent; heights are drawn about 8x exaggerated so planes clear the globe and stay visible; the turnaround is a short fixed pause.
